Makassar

Makassar, the provincial capital of South Sulawesi, Indonesia, serves as a critical nexus between the inner archipelago and the wider Indian Ocean world. Situated on the southwestern coast of Sulawesi, it commands a natural harbor that has remained one of the deepest and most sheltered in the region for centuries. While it was surpassed in administrative importance by Jakarta in the early 20th century and later by regional economic hubs, Makassar retains its status as the largest city on the Indonesian island of Sulawesi. The city is not merely a modern metropolis; it is a layered urban fabric where the architecture of the 17th-century Gowa Kingdom, the infrastructure of the Dutch colonial period, and the bustling commerce of the modern era coexist. Its historical significance lies in its role as the primary port of the Gowa-Tallo Confederation, which controlled maritime trade in the eastern Indonesian archipelago, making it a pivotal node in the global exchange of spices, textiles, and ideas during the early modern period. Today, it functions as the logistical gateway for the eastern half of Indonesia, anchoring the maritime connectivity of the "Greater Indonesia" vision.

Geographic and Urban Layout

Geographically, Makassar is situated on a low-lying coastal plain adjacent to the Makassar Strait, which separates Sulawesi from Kalimantan. The city’s topography is generally flat, with slight elevations towards the north and east where the land rises gently toward the central hills of the island. This geographic position provided natural protection from the open seas to the south and west, while the strait offered a navigable route into the Celebes Sea. The modern urban center is centered around the original trading area, known historically as the Pamontangan, which is now the city’s commercial and administrative heart.

The city’s expansion has been largely radial, growing outward from the historic core of Ujung Pandang, the former name of the city center. Today, Makassar is the core of the Greater Makassar agglomeration, which includes the city of Makassar, the regencies of Maros, Takalar, and Gowa, and the city of Sungguminasa (a major administrative area within Gowa). The total population of this agglomeration exceeds 4 million people, making it one of the fastest-growing urban clusters in Indonesia. The urban area is characterized by a high density of residential and commercial activity, with the Port of Makassar serving as the primary logistical gateway for the eastern half of Indonesia.

The city faces significant environmental challenges typical of rapidly urbanizing coastal areas. Land subsidence, caused by the extraction of groundwater and the weight of new construction, has exacerbated flooding during the monsoon season (November to March). Waste management is another critical issue; the city struggles with processing the massive volume of solid waste generated by its dense population, leading to open-air dumping sites that threaten local waterways. Furthermore, the Bay of Makassar, which historically facilitated trade, now faces pollution pressures from industrial discharge, domestic sewage, and urban runoff. Despite these challenges, the city continues to modernize, with recent infrastructure projects focusing on coastal roads, public transit systems like the Trans-Makassar rapid transit, and the revitalization of historic waterfront areas to promote tourism.

The Gowa-Tallo Confederation and Pre-Colonial History

Before the arrival of European powers, the area around Makassar was dominated by the Gowa and Tallo kingdoms, which formed a powerful confederation in the late 15th and early 16th centuries. Gowa, located in the hills north of the current city center, and Tallo, on the southern peninsula, were distinct political entities that unified under a shared monarch. The title of the ruler was Arung Mattawang or Karaeng, later evolving into the Sultanate system. The capital of the Gowa-Tallo alliance was moved to Makassar (then known as Somba Opu, meaning "Place of the Ruler") in the late 15th century, a strategic decision that capitalized on the city’s superior harbor facilities and central location for maritime trade.

Under the rule of Sultan Alauddin (r. 1593–1611), the first Muslim ruler of Gowa, the confederation underwent a transformative expansion. Alauddin converted to Islam in 1605, a move that aligned the kingdom with the broader Islamic trade networks stretching from Aceh to the Malabar Coast and beyond. This religious shift was not merely spiritual but economic; it facilitated smoother trade relations with Muslim merchants and enhanced the kingdom’s prestige in the Indian Ocean world. The Gowa-Tallo state became a maritime empire, exerting influence over the surrounding islands, including parts of Kalimantan, Maluku, and the northern coast of Sulawesi. The kingdom’s power was derived from its control of the spice trade, particularly cloves and nutmeg from the Moluccas, which it acted as an intermediary for.

The political structure of Gowa-Tallo was complex, featuring a council of nobles (datu) who advised the Sultan. The Arung Lompong was the commander of the navy, which was the primary instrument of Gowa’s power. The fleet, consisting of pinisi and other types of wooden vessels, was renowned for its speed and maneuverability, enabling Gowa to project power across the archipelago. This period saw the construction of the Somba Opu, the royal palace complex, which remains the most significant archaeological site of the pre-colonial era, located just north of the modern city. The city was a cosmopolitan hub where traders from Japan, China, Malaya, and the Middle East exchanged goods, creating a vibrant cultural mosaic that predated European arrival by centuries.

The Makassar Wars and Dutch Intervention

The rise of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in the early 17th century challenged Gowa-Tallo’s dominance. The VOC sought a monopoly over the spice trade and viewed Makassar’s role as an open port and trading hub as a threat to their commercial interests. Tensions escalated in the 1630s, leading to a series of conflicts known as the Makassar Wars.

The first phase of conflict began in 1635, but it was the second war, culminating in the Siege of Makassar in 1666–1667, that proved decisive. The conflict was exacerbated by internal divisions within the Gowa-Tallo confederation. Arung Palakka, the ruler of Bone (a vassal state of Gowa), allied with the Dutch and Bugis forces to challenge Gowa’s supremacy. The Dutch, under the command of Admiral Cornelis Speelman, provided artillery and naval support to the Bone forces, while Gowa was isolated as other regional powers remained neutral or supported the Dutch to curb Gowa’s growing power. The siege was brutal, involving naval bombardments and land assaults that devastated the city’s defenses.

The Treaty of Makassar, signed on September 12, 1667, marked the end of Gowa’s independence. The treaty stripped the Sultan of his naval power, forced the abandonment of the island fortress of Somba Opu, and allowed the Dutch to build their own fort, Fort Rotterdam, in the city center. The treaty also granted the Bugis, particularly the Bone and Wajo regents, significant political and economic privileges in Makassar, effectively turning the city into a polycentric polity where Dutch, Bugis, and remaining Gowa interests coexisted under a fragile balance of power. This period established the multicultural character of Makassar, with Bugis, Mandar, and Malay populations becoming integral parts of the urban fabric. The destruction of the royal palace and the imposition of Dutch fortifications signaled a shift from a sovereign maritime empire to a colonial dependency.

Colonial Era and the Rise of the Port

Following the defeat of the Gowa-Tallo state, Makassar remained a significant but diminished port under Dutch colonial rule. The VOC and later the Dutch East Indies government maintained Fort Rotterdam as the administrative and military center. The city’s economy shifted from a hub of independent royal trade to a service port for the Dutch colonial administration, focusing on the export of agricultural products from the interior, such as coffee, tobacco, and rice.

The 19th century saw gradual modernization of the port facilities. The construction of better warehouses and the improvement of road networks connected the port to the hinterland. However, Makassar’s relative importance waned compared to Surabaya, which became the primary port for Java and the eastern colonies. The Dutch invested heavily in Surabaya’s infrastructure, including the development of the Tanjung Perak harbor, which offered deeper water and more efficient rail links. Despite this, Makassar retained its cultural significance as a center of Islamic scholarship and Bugis-Makassar maritime tradition.

The arrival of the Japanese during World War II (1942–1945) further disrupted the city’s economy. The Japanese occupation was marked by forced labor (romusha) and the requisitioning of resources for the war effort. The liberation of Indonesia in 1945 saw Makassar become a battleground for Indonesian nationalists and Dutch forces attempting to reassert control. The city suffered from heavy bombing and street fighting, which damaged much of the historic architecture. The post-war period saw the city integrate into the newly independent Republic of Indonesia, with its status as a regional capital reaffirmed. In the 1960s, under President Sukarno and later Suharto, the city underwent significant state-led modernization, including the renaming of the city from Ujung Pandang to Makassar in 1971, a move symbolizing a reconnection with its historical identity.

Socio-Cultural Identity and the Bugis-Makassar Diaspora

Makassar’s cultural identity is deeply rooted in the interplay between its indigenous Bugis and Makassar (Makassarese) communities. The Bugis people, originally from the central highlands of South Sulawesi, migrated to the coastal plains and the Makassar area in large numbers, particularly after the fall of Gowa-Tallo. Over centuries, Bugis and Makassar cultures merged, creating a distinct urban identity that is neither purely Bugis nor purely Makassar but a hybrid synthesis.

This cultural fusion is evident in the language, where Makassar Malay serves as the lingua franca, heavily influenced by Bugis and Arabic vocabulary. The cuisine of Makassar is renowned for its spice profiles, with dishes like Coto Makassar (a beef soup with rich spice paste), Konro (beef ribs cooked in dark syrup and spices), and Pallumara (chicken soup) being staples of local hospitality. The city is also known for its traditional textiles, particularly Songket, which features intricate gold thread patterns and is often worn during formal ceremonies.

The most striking aspect of Makassar’s cultural influence, however, is its massive diaspora. The Bugis-Makassar people are one of the largest seafaring diasporas in the world, with significant communities in Indonesia (Sumatra, Java, Kalimantan), Malaysia, Singapore, and the Philippines. This diaspora is driven by centuries of maritime trade and agricultural migration. In modern times, thousands of Bugis and Makassar migrants have settled in Kuala Lumpur and other Southeast Asian cities, forming vibrant communities that preserve their language, customs, and culinary traditions. This transnational network facilitates trade, cultural exchange, and political influence, making Makassar a soft-power hub in the Malay Archipelago. The diaspora also contributes significantly to the city’s economy through remittances and business connections.

Modern Economy and Logistics

In the contemporary era, Makassar has evolved into a major economic engine for Eastern Indonesia. It is the primary entry point for goods, raw materials, and tourists heading to the islands of Sulawesi, Maluku, and Papua. The Port of Makassar is one of the busiest in the country, handling millions of tons of cargo annually. The city is the headquarters for many major Indonesian corporations, including shipping lines, energy companies, and telecommunications providers.

The city’s economic diversification is evident in its growing tourism sector. Makassar serves as the gateway to world-class natural attractions, including the coral reefs of the Bone and Bantimurung caves, the historic sites of Gowa, and the beaches of Maros. The government has invested in tourism infrastructure, including hotels, marinas, and cultural centers, to attract both domestic and international visitors. The Festival Pinisi and other cultural events highlight the city’s maritime heritage, drawing attention to the traditional pinisi boat-building industry, which is a UNESCO-recognized intangible cultural heritage.

However, the city faces economic disparities. While the central and northern parts of the city enjoy modern amenities and high standards of living, the southern coastal areas still struggle with poverty, limited access to clean water, and inadequate sanitation. Addressing these inequalities is a key challenge for the local government as it seeks to balance rapid industrial growth with social sustainability.

Urban Challenges and Future Development

Makassar is currently undergoing a significant transformation as it prepares for its role as the main hub for the new Indonesian capital, Nusantara, which is being built in East Kalimantan. Due to its strategic location, Makassar is expected to serve as the secondary administrative center and the primary logistical support base for the new capital. This designation has accelerated investment in infrastructure, including the expansion of the Makassar International Airport and the development of high-speed rail links connecting the city to the port.

The city is also grappling with the challenge of urban heat islands and air quality, exacerbated by vehicle emissions and industrial activity. To address this, the municipal government has launched initiatives to increase green spaces, promote public transportation, and enforce stricter environmental regulations. The Makassar Green City project aims to integrate ecological sustainability into urban planning, focusing on mangrove restoration along the coast to combat erosion and mitigate flood risks.

Education and innovation are also central to Makassar’s future. The presence of several major universities, including Hasanuddin University, one of the oldest and largest in Indonesia, positions the city as a center for higher education and research. The city is working to foster a startup ecosystem, particularly in the digital and creative sectors, leveraging its young and educated population to drive economic growth beyond traditional trade and agriculture.
