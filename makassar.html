Makassar

Makassar, the provincial capital of South Sulawesi, Indonesia, serves as a critical nexus between the inner archipelago and the wider Indian Ocean world. Situated on the southwestern coast of Sulawesi, it commands a natural harbor that has remained one of the deepest and most sheltered in the region for centuries. While it was surpassed in administrative importance by Jakarta in the early 20th century and later by regional economic hubs, Makassar retains its status as the largest city on the Indonesian island of Sulawesi and the second-largest on the island of Borneo. The city is not merely a modern metropolis; it is a layered urban fabric where the architecture of the 17th-century Gowa Kingdom, the infrastructure of the Dutch colonial period, and the bustling commerce of the modern era coexist. Its historical significance lies in its role as the primary port of the Gowa-Tallo Confederation, which controlled maritime trade in the eastern Indonesian archipelago, making it a pivotal node in the global exchange of spices, textiles, and ideas during the early modern period.

Geographic and Urban Layout

Geographically, Makassar is situated on a low-lying coastal plain adjacent to the Makassar Strait, which separates Sulawesi from Kalimantan. The city’s topography is generally flat, with slight elevations towards the north and east where the land rises gently toward the central hills of the island. This geographic position provided natural protection from the open seas to the south and west, while the strait offered a navigable route into the Celebes Sea. The modern urban center is centered around the original trading area, known historically as the *Pamontangan*, which is now the city’s commercial and administrative heart.

The city’s expansion has been largely radial, growing outward from the historic core of Ujung Pandang, the former name of the city center. Today, Makassar is a sprawling agglomeration that includes the cities of Makassar and Maros, as well as the regencies of Takalar and Gowa. The urban area is characterized by a high density of residential and commercial activity, with the port of Soekarno-Hatta serving as the primary logistical gateway for the eastern half of Indonesia. The city faces significant environmental challenges typical of rapidly urbanizing coastal areas, including land subsidence, flooding during the monsoon season, and waste management issues. The Bay of Makassar, which historically facilitated trade, now faces pollution pressures from industrial discharge and urban runoff, necessitating ongoing municipal efforts to preserve the ecological balance of the harbor.

The Gowa-Tallo Confederation and Pre-Colonial History

Before the arrival of European powers, the area around Makassar was dominated by the Gowa and Tallo kingdoms, which formed a confederation in the 14th and 15th centuries. Gowa, located in the hills north of the current city center, and Tallo, on the southern peninsula, were distinct political entities that unified under a shared monarch, the *Arungpalaka*, though the dynastic lines remained distinct. The capital of the Gowa-Tallo alliance was moved to Makassar in the late 15th century, a strategic decision that capitalized on the city’s superior harbor facilities.

Under the rule of Sultan Alauddin (r. 1593–1611), the first Muslim ruler of Gowa, the confederation underwent a transformative expansion. Alauddin converted to Islam, a move that aligned the kingdom with the broader Islamic trade networks stretching from Aceh to the Malabar Coast. This religious shift was not merely spiritual but economic; it facilitated smoother trade relations with Muslim merchants and enhanced the kingdom’s prestige. The Gowa-Tallo state became a maritime empire, exerting influence over the surrounding islands, including parts of Kalimantan, Maluku, and the northern coast of Sulawesi. The kingdom’s power was derived from its control of the spice trade, particularly cloves and nutmeg from the Moluccas, which it acted as a intermediary for.

The political structure of Gowa-Tallo was complex, featuring a council of nobles (*datu*) who advised the Sultan. The *Arung Lompong* was the commander of the navy, which was the primary instrument of Gowa’s power. The fleet, consisting of *pinisi* and other types of wooden vessels, was renowned for its speed and maneuverability, enabling Gowa to project power across the archipelago. This period saw the construction of the Somba Opu, the royal palace, which remains the most significant archaeological site of the pre-colonial era, located just north of the modern city.

The Makassar Wars and Dutch Intervention

The rise of the Dutch East India Company (VOC) in the early 17th century challenged Gowa-Tallo’s dominance. The VOC sought a monopoly over the spice trade and viewed Makassar’s role as an open port and trading hub as a threat to their commercial interests. Tensions escalated in the 1630s, leading to a series of conflicts known as the Makassar Wars.

The first phase of conflict began in 1635, but it was the second war, culminating in the Siege of Makassar in 1666–1667, that proved decisive. The conflict was exacerbated by internal divisions within the Gowa-Tallo confederation. Arung Palakka, the ruler of Bone (a vassal state of Gowa), allied with the Dutch and Bugis forces to challenge Gowa’s supremacy. The Dutch, under the command of Cornelis Speelman, provided artillery and naval support to the Bugis-Gowa alliance against the Sultan. The siege resulted in the destruction of the Makassar forts, *Somba Opu* remained partially intact but was no longer a fortified royal seat, and the city’s economy was severely disrupted.

The Treaty of Makassar, signed on September 12, 1667, marked the end of Gowa’s independence. The treaty stripped the Sultan of his naval power, forced the abandonment of the island fortress of Somba Opu, and allowed the Dutch to build their own fort, Fort Rotterdam, in the city center. The treaty also granted the Bugis, particularly the Bone and Wajo regents, significant political and economic privileges in Makassar, effectively turning the city into a polycentric polity where Dutch, Bugis, and remaining Gowa interests coexisted under a fragile balance of power. This period established the multicultural character of Makassar, with Bugis, Mandar, and Malay populations becoming integral parts of the urban fabric.

Colonial Era and the Rise of the Port

Following the defeat of the Gowa-Tallo state, Makassar remained a significant but diminished port under Dutch colonial rule. The VOC and later the Dutch East Indies government maintained Fort Rotterdam as the administrative and military center. The city’s economy shifted from a hub of independent royal trade to a service port for the Dutch colonial administration, focusing on the export of agricultural products from the interior, such as coffee, tobacco, and rice.

The 19th century saw gradual modernization of the port facilities. The construction of better warehouses and the improvement of road networks connected the port to the hinterland. However, Makassar’s relative importance waned compared to Surabaya, which became the primary port for Java and the eastern colonies. The Dutch invested heavily in Surabaya’s infrastructure, including the development of the Tanjung Perak harbor, which offered deeper water and more efficient rail links. Despite this, Makassar retained its cultural significance as a center of Islamic scholarship and Bugis-Makassar maritime tradition.

The arrival of the Japanese during World War II (1942–1945) further disrupted the city’s economy. The Japanese occupation was marked by forced labor and the requisitioning of resources for the war effort. The liberation of Indonesia in 1945 saw Makassar become a battleground for Indonesian nationalists and Dutch forces attempting to reassert control. The city suffered from heavy bombing and street fighting, which damaged much of the historic architecture. The post-war period saw the city integrate into the newly independent Republic of Indonesia, with its status as a regional capital reaffirmed.

Socio-Cultural Identity and the Bugis-Makassar Diaspora

Makassar’s cultural identity is deeply rooted in the interplay between its indigenous Bugis and Makassar (Makassar/Makassarese) communities. The Bugis people, originally from the central highlands of South Sulawesi, migrated to the coastal plains and the Makassar area in large numbers, particularly after the fall of Gowa-Tallo. Over centuries, Bugis and Makassar cultures merged, creating a distinct urban identity that is neither purely Bugis nor purely Makassar but a hybrid synthesis.

This cultural fusion is evident in the language, where Makassar Malay serves as the lingua franca, heavily influenced by Bugis and Arabic vocabulary. The cuisine of Makassar is renowned for its spice profiles, with dishes like *Coto Makassar* (a beef soup with rich spice paste), *Konro* (beef ribs), and *Pallubasa* (a noodle soup) becoming iconic symbols of the city. The *Pinisi* shipbuilding tradition, rooted in Bugis and Makassar maritime engineering, continues to thrive in the villages around the harbor, such as Sudiang and Tana Beru. These ships, known for their distinctive lateen sails and hull design, are now recognized by UNESCO as an Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

The Bugis-Makassar diaspora has historically been one of the largest in the world. Bugis traders and sailors established communities in Singapore, Malaysia, the Philippines, the Maldives, and as far away as South Africa and Indonesia’s remote islands. This diaspora carried the *Pinisi* tradition, Islamic religious practices, and commercial networks across the Indian Ocean, reinforcing Makassar’s global connections long before the age of industrial shipping. The city’s social fabric remains stratified yet interconnected, with distinct neighborhoods associated with different ethnic and religious groups, including Chinese, Arab, and Indonesian Muslims.

Modern Economy and Development

In the modern era, Makassar has emerged as a vital economic hub for eastern Indonesia. The city serves as the primary entry point for goods, people, and capital into the region. The port of Makassar is one of the busiest in Indonesia, handling a significant volume of containerized cargo, bulk commodities, and passenger traffic. The development of the Trans-Sulawesi Highway has improved connectivity with other parts of the island, facilitating the movement of agricultural products, such as rice from South Sulawesi, which is a major rice-producing region.

The city’s economy is diverse, including trade, logistics, manufacturing, and services. The growth of the real estate sector has been notable, with high-rise apartments and commercial towers rising in the city center. However, this rapid development has also brought challenges, including traffic congestion, urban sprawl, and housing affordability issues. The government has invested in infrastructure projects, such as the Makassar New Port, designed to increase the port’s capacity and reduce congestion at the main harbor.

Tourism is another growing sector, driven by the city’s historical sites, cultural festivals, and proximity to the Taka Bonerate National Park and the coral reefs of Spermonde Archipelago. The *Pinisi* festival and the *Makassar Festival* attract both domestic and international visitors. Despite these developments, Makassar faces ongoing issues with urban governance, including waste management, water supply, and environmental sustainability. The city’s position as a gateway to the eastern archipelago ensures its continued strategic importance in Indonesia’s national development plan.

Open Questions and Historical Debates

Several historical and contemporary questions remain open regarding Makassar’s past and future. One area of scholarly debate is the precise nature of the political structure of the Gowa-Tallo confederation. While it is widely accepted that Gowa and Tallo formed an alliance, the extent of their centralization and the degree of autonomy enjoyed by subject kingdoms is still being refined through archaeological and textual analysis. The role of women in the pre-colonial political structure is also an area of growing interest, with recent studies highlighting the influence of *Raja* (female rulers) in the Bugis and Makassar societies.

Another open question is the long-term impact of the 1667 Treaty of Makassar on the region’s political dynamics. While the treaty is often cited as the end of Gowa’s independence, historians debate the extent to which the Sultan retained symbolic power and how the Bugis political dominance in Makassar shaped the region’s ethnic relations in the following centuries. The current tension between Bugis and Makassar identities, though largely cultural rather than political, reflects these historical undercurrents.

Looking to the future, the challenge for Makassar is to balance economic growth with environmental sustainability. The city’s location in the Indian Ocean makes it vulnerable to rising sea levels and storm surges. The management of the Makassar Strait, a critical shipping lane, requires careful coordination between the port authorities, the military, and environmental agencies. The integration of Makassar into the broader ASEAN economic community will likely increase its importance as a logistics hub, but this will also exacerbate pressure on local resources and infrastructure.
