The Bleed

The rain in Sector 4 didn’t fall; it hovered, suspended in a grid of violet static that hummed against Tamsin Vreel’s teeth. She pressed her forehead against the cold, vibrating glass of the Cartographer drop-pod, watching the city of Aethelgard bleed into the sky. To her left, the spires of modern Aethelgard were sharp, steel needles piercing the cloud layer. To her right, intersecting at a sickening, impossible angle, were the thatched roofs of Aethelgard-Prior, a medieval settlement from a timeline that hadn’t known electricity. A cow with four necks drifted past the window, its eyes wide and vacant, chewing on a holographic billboard for synthetic coffee.

Tamsin’s hand trembled, not from fear, but from the vibration of the Anchor-Crystal resting in the inner pocket of her flight suit. It was no larger than a human heart, but it was heavy with the weight of a collapsed star. It pulsed against her ribs, a second heartbeat that felt like a warning. She could feel the membrane around her body thinning. Her fingertips had begun to lose opacity, turning translucent like old celluloid. The cost of the theft was already writing itself on her skin.

The drop-pod shuddered. A red light blinked on the console. Drones inbound. Distance: 200 meters.

Tamsin slammed the manual override. The pod’s thrusters screamed, tearing a hole in the suspended rain. She wasn’t running toward safety; she was running toward the one place the Cartographers’ maps showed as empty. A void. A glitch in the geometry.

"Come on," she whispered, her voice sounding thin, distant, as if coming from a radio in another room. She kicked the hatch release. The pod door burst open, sucked by the negative pressure of the Fracture Zone.

Tamsin didn’t jump. She folded.

It was a technique she had learned to hate, a violent crumpling of space that required more will than muscle. She grabbed the edges of the open pod, visualized the distance to the Ashmere Verge, and pushed. Space groaned. The world stretched, then snapped back. The drop-pod was left hanging in mid-air, slowly drifting downward, while Tamsin tumbled through the air, her vision blurring as her cohesion dropped to forty percent.

She hit the ground hard.

The ground was wrong. It wasn’t solid. It was a patchwork of textures: one second it was wet asphalt, the next, coarse sand, then again, the slick mud of a rice paddy. The horizon was a jagged mess of overlapping skylines, some burning with the light of a dying sun, others shrouded in eternal twilight. This was the Ashmere Verge, the blind spot in the multiverse, where the laws of physics had given up trying to govern the chaos.

Tamsin rolled, gasping, her lungs burning with air that tasted of ozone and old blood. Her left hand was almost invisible. She could see the veins beneath the skin, glowing faintly with residual spatial energy. She had minutes. Maybe less.

"You’re bleeding out of reality, Anchor."

The voice was calm, dry, and utterly unimpressed by the apocalypse unfolding around them.

Tamsin scrambled to her feet, drawing the small, illegal spatial knife from her boot. She spun to face the speaker.

He was leaning against a piece of debris that looked like the wing of a 21st-century jetliner, fused with a timber frame from a medieval barn. The man was lean, dressed in layered, mismatched clothing—cargo pants from one era, a tattered trench coat from another. His hair was white, but his face was young. His eyes were the most unsettling part: they didn’t reflect the light of the fractured sky. They seemed to look through it, into the static.

Kellan Irie. The Unmoored.

He didn’t move. He just watched her, his hands tucked into his pockets, as if she were a stray dog rather than a high-value defector carrying a stolen piece of the Stable Atlas.

"Drop the knife," Kellan said, not as a command, but as a statement of fact. "It’s going to phase out of your hand before you can swing it. You’re already fifty percent ghost."

Tamsin gritted her teeth, forcing her focus. She tried to solidify her hand, to pull her atoms back together, but the Crystal was sapping her will. "Who are you?"

"I’m the reason you didn’t land on the roof of that cathedral," Kellan said, gesturing vaguely at a spire that was currently sinking into a pool of liquid mercury. "I moved it. Three seconds ago. Before you hit."

Tamsin lowered the knife, her arms shaking. "Why?"

"Because if you died, the Cartographers would come looking. And they’d find me. I don’t like the Cartographers. They have the audacity to measure the infinite with a ruler."

Before Tamsin could respond, a sound cut through the ambient hum of the Fracture. It was a rhythmic, mechanical thumping, like a giant hammer hitting a drum. The air rippled.

Kellan’s expression hardened. He stepped away from the debris, pulling a device from his coat. It looked like a compass, but the needle was spinning wildly, pointing in three directions at once.

"Drone swarm," Kellan said. "Spatial trackers. They’re mapping your trail."

Tamsin looked up. In the distance, the sky was tearing open. Through the rift, she saw sleek, angular shapes descending, their hulls polished to a mirror shine that reflected the chaos of the Ashmere Verge. They were beautiful and terrifying, the epitome of Cartographer precision.

"We have to go," Tamsin said, the word dragging out of her as her concentration wavered.

"Not yet." Kellan didn’t sound worried. He sounded resigned. "There’s someone else here. Someone who doesn’t use drones. She uses clocks."

As if on cue, the air in front of them shimmered. The chaotic patchwork of the ground smoothed out, forming a perfect, circular circle of gray stone. In the center stood a figure wrapped in robes that seemed to be made of shadow and gold thread.

The woman was old. Her face was a map of wrinkles, but her eyes were bright, sharp, and terrifyingly young. Her hands were covered in gloves of woven silver, but Tamsin could see the dust falling from the fingers, crumbling away like ash.

"Penultima," Kellan said, his voice tight. "You’re early."

The old woman stepped forward. Each step left a faint, glowing footprint that faded after a second. She looked at Tamsin, and the temperature in the Ashmere Verge dropped ten degrees.

"The Anchor-Crystal," Penultima said. Her voice sounded like pages turning in a silent library. "You have it, child. And you are losing your shape."

Tamsin clutched her side. "I’m not here for your temple. I’m here to burn the Atlas."

Penultima smiled. It was a thin, sad expression. "The Atlas is a cage, yes. But the alternative is a storm. Do you think the wild multiverse is safe? It is a place where memories eat each other. Where time loops until sanity breaks. I offer order. I offer a pause."

"A pause," Kellan spat. "You call it a pause. We call it a coffin."

Tamsin’s vision blurred. The edge of the world was fraying. She felt herself drifting, untethered. She looked at Kellan, then at Penultima. The Cartographer drones were close now, their engines whining in the upper atmosphere.

"Which way out?" Tamsin asked, her voice barely a whisper.

Kellan looked at the old woman, then at the sky. "There is no out. Only through. But the path is closed. The Weavers are here too. I can feel them. Splicing in."

Tamsin felt a sudden, sharp pain in her head. Not physical pain, but a memory that wasn’t hers. She saw a room with blue walls. She saw herself, older, wearing a Cartographer uniform, smiling as she pressed a button that launched a city into orbit. The guilt hit her like a physical blow, doubling her over.

This is what they do, a voice echoed in her mind. They splice the guilt in. It makes you stronger. It makes you obedient.

Kellan’s eyes widened. "Tamsin! Don’t listen! It’s a Weaver thread!"

Tamsin fell to her knees, clawing at her head. The world was dissolving into colors. The drones. The old woman. The smuggler. All of it was becoming noise, becoming data, becoming a story that could be rewritten.

You are part of the Atlas, the voice whispered. You belong here.

Tamsin’s hand, which was now almost entirely transparent, reached out. She grabbed the Anchor-Crystal. It was warm. Too warm.

Kellan moved with a speed that defied logic. He didn’t fold space; he simply was there, in front of her, his hand gripping hers. His skin was warm, solid, real.

"Look at me," he said. His voice was rough, grounded. "I am Kellan. I am lost. I am here. You are Tamsin. You are broken. But you are here."

The Weaver’s presence faltered. The false memory shuddered, cracking like ice.

Penultima watched, her crumbling hands clasped before her. "A waste of such a strong spine," she murmured.

Tamsin gasped, pulling her hand back. The guilt was still there, a heavy stone in her gut, but the external narrative was gone. She looked at her hand. It was solidifying, the translucency fading back to flesh.

"The drones," Tamsin wheezed.

"Behind us," Kellan said.

Tamsin looked up. The sky was filled with the silver shapes of the Cartographer swarm. And below them, rising from the mist, were the jagged, shadowy spires of the Weaver’s fleet.

Kellan grinned, a sharp, dangerous expression. "Well. I suppose we aren’t going to the Spire after all."

He pulled a lever on the debris behind them—a device that looked like a car engine welded to a book. "Hold on. This is going to hurt your equilibrium."

Tamsin didn’t hesitate. She grabbed the edge of the device. The Ashmere Verge lurched, the laws of physics snapping and rewiring themselves around them. The world turned upside down, and Tamsin Vreel, the defector, the ghost, the anchor, fell into the unknown, dragging her savior and her hunter into the dark.
