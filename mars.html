Mars: The Red Planet

Mars, the fourth planet from the Sun, is the second-smallest planet in the Solar System after Mercury. Named after the Roman god of war, its distinct reddish appearance—often leading to the moniker "The Red Planet"—results from iron oxide (rust) prevalent on its surface. With a diameter of approximately 6,779 kilometers (4,212 miles), Mars is roughly half the size of Earth, yet it possesses geological features that dwarf anything on our own planet. Orbiting at an average distance of 227.9 million kilometers (141.6 million miles), it completes one revolution around the Sun every 687 Earth days, or 1.88 Earth years. Although its current environment is cold, dry, and radiation-heavy, Mars is the most active target for robotic exploration in the Solar System. Scientific inquiry is driven by strong evidence that Mars once hosted a warmer, wetter climate capable of supporting microbial life, making it a critical comparative window into planetary evolution, atmospheric dynamics, and the potential for future human habitation.

Discovery and Early Observation

Mars has been visible to the naked eye since prehistoric times, meaning its "discovery" predates recorded history. However, the systematic scientific study of the planet began in the late 17th century. While Galileo Galilei was the first to observe Mars through a telescope in 1610, his primary contribution was confirming that the planet exhibits phases similar to the Moon, proving it orbits the Sun. It was not until 1659 that Christiaan Huygens observed the polar caps, and in 1877, Italian astronomer Giovanni Schiaparelli made detailed observations during a favorable opposition. Schiaparelli mapped numerous dark lineaments which he called canali (channels). This term was mistranslated into English as "canals," sparking a decades-long public fascination and the myth of Martian civilizations.

This misconception was largely popularized by American astronomer Percival Lowell, who published a series of books in the early 20th century arguing that these lines were artificial structures built by Martians to transport water. It was not until the era of spaceflight that these features were revealed to be optical illusions caused by the low resolution of early telescopes and the planet’s atmospheric haze. The first spacecraft to successfully fly by Mars was the Soviet Union’s Mars 1 in 1963, though it lost contact before completing its mission. The United States’ Mariner 4 in 1965 provided the first close-up images from orbit, revealing a cratered, barren surface devoid of canals, effectively ending the "green Mars" era of speculation.

Physical Characteristics and Geology

Mars is a terrestrial planet composed primarily of silicate rock, featuring a core, mantle, and crust similar in structure to Earth but with distinct differences. The Martian core is estimated to be an iron-nickel alloy with a significant proportion of light elements like sulfur and oxygen. Unlike Earth, Mars lacks a globally active magnetic field. Seismic data from the InSight lander suggests that the core is partially molten and significantly larger in proportion to the planet’s radius than Earth’s core, which likely explains the cessation of a global dynamo effect early in Martian history.

The most prominent geological feature on Mars is the Tharsis bulge, a massive volcanic plateau covering a large portion of the equatorial region. This area contains several of the largest volcanoes in the Solar System, including Olympus Mons. Olympus Mons is a shield volcano with a summit approximately 21.9 kilometers (13.6 miles) above the surrounding plain, nearly 2.5 times the height of Mount Everest. Its base is roughly the size of the U.S. state of Arizona. The formation of such immense structures is attributed to the lack of plate tectonics on Mars; unlike Earth, where volcanoes move as plates shift over hotspots, Martian volcanoes remained in a single location, building up height over millions of years.

The planet’s surface is heavily cratered, particularly in the southern hemisphere, which is older and more rugged, while the northern hemisphere is characterized by smoother, darker, and younger terrain. The most significant impact feature is the Hellas Planitia in the southern hemisphere, a depression approximately 2,300 kilometers in diameter and up to 7 kilometers deep. Another major feature is the Valles Marineris, a vast canyon system stretching 4,000 kilometers and reaching depths of 7 kilometers. Recent modeling suggests Valles Marineris was formed primarily by tectonic stress from the underlying Tharsis bulge causing the crust to fracture and collapse, rather than by fluid erosion alone. Additionally, the planet exhibits clear evidence of ancient riverbeds, deltaic deposits, and alluvial fans, strongly suggesting that liquid water once flowed on the surface extensively.

Atmospheric Composition and Evolution

The Martian atmosphere is extremely thin, with a surface pressure of only about 600 pascals, less than 1% of Earth’s surface pressure. This thinness results from the loss of the global magnetic field, which allowed the solar wind to strip away a significant portion of the upper atmosphere over billions of years. The air is roughly 95.3% carbon dioxide, 2.7% nitrogen and 1.6% argon, with traces of oxygen, carbon monoxide and water vapor.

Due to this thin atmosphere, Mars experiences extreme temperature fluctuations. The mean global temperature is approximately -63°C (-81°F). However, local temperatures can range from -153°C (-243°F) at the polar winter nights to as high as 20°C (68°F) in the summer afternoons near the equator. The lack of a substantial greenhouse effect means the planet has little ability to retain heat, resulting in a highly variable thermal profile.

Despite its coldness, Mars exhibits dynamic weather patterns. Dust storms are a common phenomenon, ranging from small, localized eddies to planet-wide events that can obscure sunlight and last for weeks or months. These storms are driven by strong winds generated by seasonal temperature gradients and the sublimation of the polar carbon dioxide caps. During winter, carbon dioxide freezes out of the atmosphere to form seasonal caps; in spring, as these caps sublimate, they drive strong winds that can loft fine dust particles into the upper atmosphere. The 2018 global dust storm, which obscured the Sun and reduced power generation for rovers, highlighted the severity of these events. The permanent polar caps contain significant quantities of water ice, estimated to hold enough water to cover the entire surface of Mars to a depth of several meters if melted.

Magnetic Field Dynamics and Space Weather

While Mars currently lacks a global magnetic field, geological evidence indicates it possessed one as recently as 4 billion years ago. The presence of magnetized rocks in the southern highlands, mapped by missions such as the Mars Global Surveyor and MAVEN, suggests that the planet’s core was once large enough and rotating fast enough to generate a dynamo effect. This global field protected the early Martian atmosphere from solar wind stripping, allowing for a thicker atmosphere that could have supported liquid water on the surface.

The transition from a magnetized to an unmagnetized state is a critical area of study regarding planetary habitability. Theories suggest that the magnetic field collapsed as the core began to solidify or as the core’s composition changed, reducing its ability to sustain convection necessary for the dynamo. Once the field disappeared, the solar wind began to erode the upper atmosphere. The MAVEN (Mars Atmosphere and Volatile Evolution) mission, currently in orbit, has provided detailed measurements of this escape process, indicating that solar wind stripping has removed roughly half of the Martian atmosphere since the field’s collapse.

Today, Mars exhibits crustal magnetic fields—localized magnetic anomalies frozen in the rocks of the southern hemisphere. These fields interact with the solar wind, creating complex magnetic drapes and ion foreshocks. Understanding these interactions is vital for assessing the radiation environment for future human explorers, as the lack of a global field leaves the surface exposed to galactic cosmic rays and solar energetic particles.

Hydrology and Potential for Life

The search for water on Mars is the primary driver of modern exploration missions. Since the 1990s, multiple orbiters and landers have provided conclusive evidence of ancient water. The Mars Exploration Rovers, Spirit and Opportunity, landed in 2004 and found mineralogical evidence of past water, including hematite (rust) and sulfate salts, which typically form in the presence of liquid water. The Curiosity rover, which landed in Gale Crater in 2012, further confirmed that Mars hosted a habitable environment billions of years ago. Curiosity’s findings indicate that an ancient lake existed in Gale Crater, with pH levels and mineral compositions similar to neutral, non-toxic lakes on Earth.

More recently, the Perseverance rover, which landed in Jezero Crater in February 2021, has been collecting and caching rock samples from an ancient river delta for future return to Earth. This mission is part of the NASA-ESA Mars Sample Return program, which aims to bring these pristine samples back for detailed laboratory analysis. The discovery of subsurface water ice, confirmed by the MARSIS radar instrument on the Mars Express orbiter and the SHARAD radar on MRO, suggests that vast reserves of water exist beneath the surface, particularly at the poles and high latitudes, protected from the harsh radiation and low temperatures of the open environment.

While no direct evidence of current or past life has been found, the preservation of organic molecules in rock samples is a major priority. The combination of ancient habitable environments, the presence of organic precursors, and the preservation of geological records makes Mars the most promising candidate in the Solar System for extraterrestrial life.

Space Exploration Missions

Robotic exploration of Mars has been a cornerstone of space agency activities since the 1960s. The Soviet Union and the United States led the early efforts, with the Mariner series (1965–1972) and the Viking program (1975–1976) establishing the baseline knowledge of the planet. The Viking missions were the first to conduct biological experiments, searching for microbial life. The results were ambiguous and remain a subject of debate, with most scientists concluding that the detected signals were abiotic chemical reactions, although some researchers argue they may indicate past biological activity.

The European Space Agency’s Mars Express orbiter, launched in 2003, has been continuously mapping the surface and studying the atmosphere, providing long-duration data on seasonal changes. NASA’s Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO), launched in 2005, serves as the premier high-resolution imaging platform, providing global maps with resolution down to 0.3 meters. In 2018, NASA’s InSight lander deployed seismometers and a heat flow probe to study the planet’s interior structure, revealing the size of the core and the frequency of "marsquakes."

Current operations are dominated by the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers, both part of the Mars Science Laboratory and Mars 2020 programs, respectively. Looking ahead, the field is shifting toward crewed exploration. NASA’s Artemis program, focused on the Moon, is a stepping stone to Mars, aiming to establish a lunar base by the 2030s. The Space Launch System (SLS) and Starship vehicles are being developed to transport astronauts and cargo to the Martian surface. The primary challenges remain the long transit time (6–9 months), the harsh radiation environment, and the need for in-situ resource utilization (ISRU) to produce oxygen and propellant from the Martian atmosphere and soil.
