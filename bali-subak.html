Subak: The Temple-Based Water Management System of Bali

Subak is a unique, water-sharing cooperative system that manages the rice terraces of Bali, Indonesia, and serves as a living example of a complex, human-engineered socio-religious organization. Recognized by UNESCO as a World Heritage Site in 2012, subak is not merely an agricultural technique but a cultural ecosystem that integrates irrigation, religious belief, and communal governance. The system operates on the principle of "tripitaka," or the interplay of the three essential elements: water, the temple, and the community. Unlike centralized bureaucratic irrigation models common in many parts of the world, subak is a decentralized, voluntary association of farmers who collectively maintain canals, allocate water based on crop schedules, and organize religious rituals to ensure agricultural fertility and social cohesion. The system demonstrates a sophisticated understanding of hydraulic engineering and social psychology, allowing it to adapt to environmental fluctuations and political changes over more than a millennium. While primarily associated with wet-rice cultivation, the principles of subak have influenced other forms of resource management on the island, highlighting the enduring relevance of communal stewardship in the face of ecological and economic pressures.

Historical Origins and Development

The origins of subak are deeply rooted in the early development of Hindu-Balinese culture, with archaeological evidence suggesting its presence as early as the 9th century CE. While the exact date of its formalization is debated among historians, the system predates the modern Balinese state. Inscriptions and temple reliefs, such as those at the Pura Besakih complex, indicate that water management was already a critical aspect of statecraft and religious life during the Old Kingdoms of Bali. The system likely evolved from earlier, simpler water-sharing arrangements used by Javanese traders and settlers who migrated to the island in the early centuries of the common era.

A pivotal moment in the history of subak was the arrival of Indian scholars and the spread of Hinduism, which provided the philosophical and ritual framework necessary to sustain such a complex cooperative. The concept of *dharma* (duty) and the importance of maintaining cosmic order (*rta*) were integrated into water management, making irrigation a spiritual obligation rather than just an economic necessity. By the 11th century, the system was well-established, as evidenced by the "Book of Law" (Kutara Manava) and other legal texts that outlined penalties for water theft and canal negligence. The system survived the decline of the Majapahit Empire in the 15th century and the subsequent period of political fragmentation, proving its resilience. It continued to evolve through the colonial period, although Dutch colonizers often misunderstood its communal nature, attempting to impose individual land tenure laws that clashed with the collective ethos of subak.

The Organizational Structure and Governance

Subak is organized as a voluntary association of farmers who own land within a specific irrigation network. There is no single central authority for all subaks on the island; instead, thousands of independent subaks operate autonomously. As of recent estimates, there are approximately 1,200 to 1,500 active subaks in Bali, ranging in size from a few hectares to over 100 hectares. Each subak is typically centered around a water temple (*Pura Ulun Danu*), which is considered the guardian of the water source. The governance structure is hierarchical yet flat, relying on consensus and tradition rather than legal contracts.

At the heart of subak governance is the *pengulu*, or head of the subak. This leader is not elected by vote but is chosen through a process of consensus, often from among the senior farmers or those with specific ritual qualifications. The *pengulu* is responsible for managing the water schedule, maintaining the canals, and organizing religious ceremonies. Assisting the *pengulu* is the *penyarik* or *bendahara* (treasurer) and the *pengawak* (accountant). Decisions are made in council meetings called *pengawak*, where all members have a voice, but the *pengulu* holds the final say based on customary law (*awig-awig*). These rules are unwritten but strictly enforced through social pressure and ritual sanctions. The system relies on transparency; the accounts of the subak, including dues and maintenance costs, are publicly displayed, ensuring accountability.

Hydraulic Engineering and Water Allocation

The physical infrastructure of subak is a marvel of low-tech hydraulic engineering. The system begins at the *bali* (the water source), usually a spring or a river, and is controlled by a dam or weir called the *sakeh*. From here, the main canal (*pari utama*) branches out into smaller distributaries (*pari alit*) that feed the individual rice fields. The design of these canals is precise, with gradients carefully calculated to ensure gravity-fed flow without excessive erosion or sedimentation.

Water allocation is managed through a rigorous scheduling system known as *tumpang sari*. This system ensures that water is distributed equitably among farmers based on the area of their fields and the stage of the rice crop cycle. Rice is divided into three main stages: *tumpang* (transplanting), *sari* (growing), and *padam* (harvesting). Farmers are grouped into classes based on these stages. Water is released to the *tumpang* group first, as they need a constant, gentle flow for transplantation. Once they are ready, they move to the next class, and so on. This rotational system, managed by the *pengulu* and the water gatekeepers (*jaga parit*), prevents upstream farmers from hoarding water and ensures that every field receives the necessary amount at the critical times. The system also includes mechanisms for managing sediment and weeds, which are removed during the dry season to prevent blockage and maintain canal depth.

Religious and Ritual Dimensions

The spiritual dimension of subak is inseparable from its practical function. In the Balinese Hindu worldview, water is a sacred element associated with the goddess Dewi Danu. The water temple (*Pura Ulun Danu*) is not merely a symbolic center but a functional node in the irrigation network. Rituals performed at these temples are believed to ensure the purity and abundance of the water, which in turn guarantees a bountiful harvest. The most important ritual is the *Odalan*, the anniversary of the temple, during which offerings of flowers, incense, and livestock are presented to the goddess.

Another critical ritual is the *Pengironan*, a ceremony performed by the farmers themselves to bless the water and the rice paddies. This reinforces the social bond between the farmers and their environment. The integration of religion serves a practical social function: it creates a sense of shared destiny and mutual obligation. Breaking the rules of subak is not just an economic violation but a religious transgression, subjecting the offender to social ostracism and spiritual consequences. This dual moral framework provides a strong incentive for compliance, reducing the need for external enforcement. The rituals also serve as a time for community gatherings, allowing for the resolution of disputes and the reinforcement of social norms.

However, it was the work of the American anthropologist David L. Steinberg (often cited in the context of Balinese agrarian studies) and, more prominently, Ralph L. B. Notable Examples and UNESCO Recognition

The Ubud Subak is one of the most famous examples of the system, located in the central part of the island near the town of Ubud. It covers approximately 600 hectares of terraced rice fields and is fed by the Tukad Petget river. The Ubud Subak is renowned for its elaborate water temples, including the Pura Taman Gili, which serves as the main ceremonial site. The terraces here are a major tourist attraction, drawing visitors from around the world who come to witness the harmonious blend of agriculture and nature.

Another notable example is the Jatiluwih Subak, located in the west of Bali, near the town of Gianyar. Jatiluwih is famous for its vast, undulating rice terraces that stretch across a valley, creating a landscape of remarkable beauty. The subak here is particularly significant because it was one of the primary sites considered for UNESCO World Heritage status. The landscape of Jatiluwih demonstrates the long-term stability of the subak system, with many of the terraces dating back several centuries. In 2012, the "Cultural Landscape of Bali Province: the Subak System as a Manifestation of the Tri Hita Karana Philosophy" was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List. This recognition highlighted the subak's role in sustaining a cultural landscape of outstanding universal value, emphasizing the philosophical concept of *Tri Hita Karana* (three causes of happiness: harmony between humans, humans and nature, and humans and the divine).

Challenges and Modern Adaptations

Despite its historical resilience, the subak system faces significant modern challenges. The most pressing issue is the encroachment of tourism and urbanization, which has led to a decline in the number of farmers and an increase in the demand for water for hotels and households. This has strained the water resources available for irrigation, leading to conflicts between agricultural and commercial users. Additionally, the aging population of farmers and the migration of young Balinese to cities have resulted in a loss of traditional knowledge and labor availability.

Climate change also poses a threat, as changing rainfall patterns and increased temperatures can disrupt the delicate water balance that the subak relies on. Droughts have become more frequent, while heavy rains can cause flooding and erosion. In response, many subaks have begun to adapt by implementing modern water-saving technologies, such as drip irrigation, and by diversifying their crops to include vegetables and fruits, which are less water-intensive than rice. Some subaks have also started to charge entry fees to tourists visiting the terraces, using the revenue for maintenance and community projects. These adaptations suggest that the subak is not a relic of the past but a living system capable of evolution, though its future sustainability remains uncertain in the face of global economic and environmental pressures.

Open Questions and Future Research

Several open questions remain regarding the long-term viability of the subak system. One key area of research is the impact of market forces on communal governance. As rice becomes a cash crop and land values rise, will the communal ethos of the subak hold, or will it fragment into individualistic land ownership? Another question is the role of technology. Can digital tools, such as mobile apps for water scheduling and drone monitoring for crop health, enhance the efficiency of the subak without undermining its social and cultural integrity?

Furthermore, the relationship between subak and local government is a subject of ongoing debate. While the Indonesian government has recognized the cultural value of subak, its legal status is still ambiguous. Farmers often face difficulties in securing land rights and water rights in the face of state regulations that favor large-scale infrastructure projects. Understanding how to integrate traditional knowledge with modern legal and scientific frameworks is a critical task for policymakers and researchers. The future of subak will depend on its ability to balance its traditional roots with the demands of a modernizing, globalized society.
