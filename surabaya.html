Surabaya

Surabaya is the capital of East Java and the most populous city in Indonesia, serving as the country’s second-largest urban center after Jakarta. Situated on the north coast of the island of Java, facing the Madura Strait, the city has long functioned as the primary commercial, industrial, and educational hub for eastern Indonesia. Known for its vibrant cultural melting pot, Surabaya’s demographics are a blend of Javanese, Chinese-Indonesian, Madurese, and Arab-Indonesian communities, reflecting centuries of maritime trade and migration. The city is an anchor of the Greater Surabaya metropolitan area, which includes Sidoarjo, Gresik, and Lamongan, and boasts one of the busiest ports in Southeast Asia. While Jakarta serves as the political seat of the nation, Surabaya holds significant economic sway, hosting a diverse range of industries including shipping, automotive, petrochemicals, and banking. The city is also steeped in a profound sense of identity and resilience, largely stemming from its pivotal role in the early stages of the Indonesian National Revolution, a legacy that is celebrated annually and remains central to the civic character of its residents, who are known locally as *Timo* or *Suroboyo*.

Historical Evolution and Colonial Foundations

The historical roots of Surabaya trace back to the 9th century, during the reign of the Medang Kingdom. Archaeological evidence, specifically the Cudaga inscription dated to 1042 AD, mentions a settlement called *Surabhaya* or *Surabaya* in the region, suggesting the existence of a significant trading port. However, the area remained relatively obscure until the arrival of European powers. In 1830, the Dutch established a formal settlement on the site of the former village of Surabaya, transforming it into a strategic military and commercial outpost. The city grew rapidly in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as the Dutch consolidated their control over the Indonesian archipelago, using Surabaya as the administrative center for East Java.

The urban layout of colonial-era Surabaya was distinct, featuring wide avenues and public squares that accommodated a growing mixed population. The *Toluk* (Dutch) and *Tiong Hoa* (Chinese) communities developed distinct neighborhoods, while the native Javanese population inhabited the surrounding areas. This period saw the construction of many of the city’s iconic landmarks, including the Ombilin Mosque (built in 1889), the Surabaya City Hall (built in 1919), and the Gedung Arsip (Archive Building). The presence of a major port facility further accelerated economic growth, making Surabaya a critical node in the spice trade and later in the export of rice, rubber, and tobacco. By the 1930s, Surabaya had solidified its status as a major urban center, though its population was still a fraction of its current size. The interplay between these colonial, Chinese, and indigenous communities laid the groundwork for the complex social dynamics that would define the city’s subsequent history.

The 1945 War and National Identity

The most defining event in Surabaya’s modern history occurred in October and November 1945, shortly after the declaration of Indonesian independence. On October 27, 1945, tensions erupted between Indonesian republican forces and British troops, who had been sent to disarm the Japanese garrison but were perceived as facilitating Dutch reinstatement. The incident, triggered by a dispute over the control of the Tunjungan flagpole, escalated into a full-scale conflict. The British, joined by Gurkha regiments, attempted to take control of the city, leading to a brutal urban war that lasted for several weeks.

The conflict reached its climax in late November 1945, when British and Allied forces launched a large-scale offensive involving artillery and airstrikes. In response, Indonesian fighters, including guerrilla units and local militias, mounted fierce resistance. The British withdrew by November 25, 1945, following heavy losses and international pressure. This series of events became a powerful symbol of Indonesian national resistance. In 1945, the Australian journalist Wilfred B. G. Smith reported on the bravery of the Indonesian defenders, and in 1946, the British Prime Minister Clement Attlee reportedly remarked that the British had "got into a shithole." More significantly, the conflict inspired the naming of the "Surabaya 10 November" holiday, which commemorates the spirit of sacrifice. In 1966, the Indonesian government officially designated November 10 as National Heroes' Day (*Hari Pahlawan*), directly referencing the resistance in Surabaya. The city’s identity became inextricably linked with this era of resistance, fostering a local culture that values toughness, resilience, and defiance against external authority.

Economic Structure and Industrial Base

Surabaya’s economy is anchored by its status as a major port city and industrial hub. The Port of Surabaya is one of the largest and busiest in Indonesia, handling a significant portion of the nation’s container traffic and bulk cargo. The port serves as a gateway for exports from East Java, Madura, and Bali, as well as imports of raw materials for local industries. The *Jawa Timur* (East Java) region benefits from its proximity to key agricultural and mining sectors, allowing Surabaya to function as a processing and distribution center.

The industrial sector in Surabaya is diverse, with key industries including automotive, petrochemicals, electronics, and textiles. Major multinational corporations, such as Toyota, Mitsubishi, and Unilever, maintain significant operations in the city and its surrounding areas, particularly in the industrial estates of Rungkut and Gedong. The automotive industry, in particular, has seen substantial growth, with several factories producing vehicles for both domestic and export markets. The city also hosts a robust financial sector, with branches of major Indonesian banks and international financial institutions, reflecting its role as a financial center for eastern Indonesia.

In recent decades, Surabaya has also seen the rise of a service economy, including tourism, hospitality, and information technology. The city’s status as an educational center, home to several prominent universities such as Airlangga University (Universitas Airlangga), contributes to its service sector. Despite the dominance of manufacturing and logistics, the service sector continues to grow, driven by an increasing middle class and improving infrastructure. The city’s economic resilience is further supported by its integration into the broader Indonesian economic framework, although it faces challenges such as labor competition and environmental pressures from industrial activity.

Urban Infrastructure and Transportation

Surabaya’s urban infrastructure has undergone significant modernization in the last two decades, aimed at accommodating its rapidly growing population and alleviating chronic traffic congestion. The city’s road network is extensive, but it has historically struggled with inefficiency and poor maintenance. To address this, the municipal government has invested heavily in public transportation and traffic management systems. One of the most notable developments is the expansion of the BRT (Bus Rapid Transit) system, known as Trans Jate, which offers a relatively affordable and efficient mode of public transport across the city’s core areas.

Air travel is facilitated by Juanda International Airport, located in the surrounding Sidoarjo regency but serving as the primary airport for Surabaya. Juanda is one of the busiest airports in Indonesia and a major hub for domestic and international flights, connecting Surabaya to key destinations in Asia, the Middle East, and Europe. The airport underwent a major expansion in the 2010s to handle increased passenger volumes and improve cargo facilities, reflecting the city’s growing importance as a regional logistics hub.

Rail transport is another critical component of Surabaya’s infrastructure. Surabaya Gubeng and Surabaya Pasarturi are the two main railway stations, serving as major nodes in the national railway network. These stations connect Surabaya to other major Javanese cities, including Jakarta, Semarang, and Banyuwangi, as well as to Bali via intercity trains. The railway system remains a popular choice for both passenger and freight transport, although it faces challenges in terms of capacity and modernization. In addition to these modes, Surabaya has been exploring the implementation of a light rail transit (LRT) system, although progress has been gradual due to funding and planning complexities. The city also maintains a network of rivers and canals, some of which have been rehabilitated for drainage and recreational purposes, such as the Sungai Surabaya (Surabaya River) revitalization project.

Demographics and Cultural Diversity

Surabaya is one of the most ethnically diverse cities in Indonesia, a reflection of its historical role as a trading port. The population is predominantly Javanese, but significant communities of Chinese-Indonesians, Madurese, and Arab-Indonesians reside in the city. The Chinese-Indonesian community, in particular, has a long history in Surabaya and plays a vital role in the city’s economic and cultural life. Many traditional Chinese businesses, temples, and cultural associations are located in areas such as Pasar Keputran and the old town. The Arab-Indonesian community, often referred to as *Wong Arab*, has also been present in Surabaya since the 18th century and is concentrated in the Kampung Arab district, known for its traditional Middle Eastern architecture and cuisine.

This cultural diversity is evident in the city’s food scene, which offers a wide range of culinary traditions. Surabaya is famous for *Rawon*, a dark beef soup with bitter bark (kluwek) flavoring, and *Lontong Balap*, a dish featuring rice cakes, vegetables, and peanut sauce. The influence of Chinese and Arab cultures is also visible in the local dialect, which includes many loanwords and has a distinctive tonal quality compared to standard Javanese. The city’s population has grown steadily, with estimates placing the metropolitan area’s population at over 3 million. This growth has driven urban expansion, leading to the development of new residential and commercial districts on the city’s periphery.

Despite the diversity, Surabaya has experienced social tensions, particularly in the late 20th century. The 1998 riots, which were part of a broader wave of anti-Chinese violence across Indonesia, caused significant damage to Chinese-owned businesses and homes in Surabaya. However, the city has since made efforts to promote social cohesion and interethnic harmony. The resilience of the local population and the shared experience of economic hardship have helped to mitigate some of the historical tensions, fostering a sense of shared civic identity that transcends ethnic boundaries.
