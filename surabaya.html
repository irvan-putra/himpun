Surabaya

Surabaya, known to its residents as Suroboyo, is the capital of East Java and the second-most populous city in Indonesia, following Jakarta. Situated on the north coast of the island of Java, facing the Madura Strait, Surabaya serves as the primary commercial, industrial, and educational hub for eastern Indonesia. As the anchor of the Greater Surabaya metropolitan area—encompassing Sidoarjo, Gresik, and Lamongan—the city boasts one of the busiest ports in Southeast Asia and acts as a critical engine for the national economy. While Jakarta holds political primacy, Surabaya exerts significant economic sway, hosting a diverse range of industries including automotive, petrochemicals, shipping, and banking.

The city’s identity is distinctively shaped by its status as a cultural melting pot, blending Javanese, Chinese-Indonesian, Madurese, and Arab-Indonesian communities. This demographic diversity is a testament to centuries of maritime trade and migration. Surabayan residents are known locally as Timo (a colloquial term derived from the city’s name) and are renowned for their resilience, directness, and fierce independence. This civic character is deeply rooted in the city’s pivotal role in the early stages of the Indonesian National Revolution, a legacy celebrated annually and central to the local psyche.

Historical Evolution and Colonial Foundations

The historical roots of Surabaya trace back to the 9th century during the reign of the Medang Kingdom (also known as Mataram Kuno). The earliest definitive record of the settlement appears in the Canggu inscription, dated to 1358 AD, which mentions Surabhaya or Surabaya as a significant trading port involved in the maritime spice trade. However, the area remained relatively obscure on the global stage until the arrival of European powers. In 1830, the Dutch established a formal colonial settlement on the site, transforming it into a strategic military and commercial outpost.

The city grew rapidly in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as the Dutch consolidated their control over the Indonesian archipelago, using Surabaya as the administrative center for East Java. The urban layout of colonial-era Surabaya was distinct, featuring wide avenues and public squares designed to accommodate a growing mixed population. The Toluk (Dutch) and Tiong Hoa (Chinese) communities developed distinct neighborhoods, while the indigenous Javanese and Madurese populations inhabited the surrounding areas. This period saw the construction of many of the city’s iconic landmarks, including the Gedung Arsip (Archive Building), the former Dutch military hospital, and various administrative structures. The presence of a major port facility accelerated economic growth, making Surabaya a critical node in the trade of rice, rubber, sugar, and tobacco. By the 1930s, Surabaya had solidified its status as a major urban center, though its population was still a fraction of its current size. The interplay between these colonial, Chinese, and indigenous communities laid the groundwork for the complex social dynamics that would define the city’s subsequent history.

The 1945 War and National Identity

The most defining event in Surabaya’s modern history occurred in October and November 1945, shortly after the declaration of Indonesian independence. On October 27, 1945, tensions erupted between Indonesian republican forces and British troops of the 23rd Indian Division, who had been sent to disarm the Japanese garrison but were perceived by locals as facilitating Dutch reinstatement. The incident, triggered by a dispute over the control of a flagpole on Tunjungan Square, escalated into a full-scale conflict.

The conflict reached its climax on November 10, 1945, when British and Allied forces launched a large-scale offensive involving artillery and airstrikes to reassert control. In response, Indonesian fighters, including guerrilla units (laskar) and local militias, mounted fierce resistance, engaging in brutal urban warfare that lasted for weeks. The battle, known as the Battle of Surabaya (Pertempuran Surabaya), resulted in heavy casualties on both sides. The British eventually withdrew, and the city became a symbol of unyielding national resistance.

The significance of this event was magnified internationally when the British Prime Minister Clement Attlee addressed the House of Commons in December 1945, lamenting that the British had "got into a shithole" (often cited in historical accounts as a reflection of the political and military quagmire) and acknowledging the extraordinary bravery of the Indonesian defenders. This narrative solidified Surabaya’s reputation as the cradle of national heroism. In 1966, the Indonesian government officially designated November 10 as Hari Pahlawan (National Heroes' Day), directly referencing the resistance in Surabaya. This commemoration is marked by solemn ceremonies, military parades, and widespread civic pride, fostering a local culture that values toughness, resilience, and defiance against external authority.

Economic Structure and Industrial Base

Surabaya’s economy is anchored by its status as a major port city and industrial hub. The Port of Surabaya is one of the largest and busiest in Indonesia, handling a significant portion of the nation’s container traffic and bulk cargo. It serves as the primary gateway for exports from East Java, Madura, and Bali, as well as imports for the region. The port’s strategic location on the north coast allows for efficient connectivity with international shipping lanes, facilitating trade with China, Japan, the Middle East, and Europe.

The industrial sector in Surabaya is diverse and robust, with key industries including automotive, petrochemicals, electronics, textiles, and food processing. Major multinational corporations maintain significant operations in the city and its surrounding regencies. For instance, the automotive industry has seen substantial growth, with assembly plants for Toyota, Mitsubishi, and Honda located in the Surabaya metropolitan area, producing vehicles for both domestic consumption and export. The petrochemical sector is also vital, particularly in the Gresik and Lamongan areas, where refineries and chemical plants process crude oil and natural gas.

In recent decades, Surabaya has experienced a rise in the service economy, including tourism, hospitality, finance, and information technology. The city hosts a robust financial sector, with branches of major Indonesian banks such as Bank Central Asia, Mandiri, and BCA, reflecting its role as a financial center for eastern Indonesia. The presence of leading educational institutions, such as Airlangga University (Universitas Airlangga), one of the most prestigious universities in Indonesia, contributes to a skilled labor force that supports the service and tech sectors. Despite the dominance of manufacturing and logistics, the service sector continues to grow, driven by an increasing middle class and improving infrastructure.

Urban Infrastructure and Transportation

Surabaya’s urban infrastructure has undergone significant modernization in the last two decades to accommodate its rapidly growing population and alleviate chronic traffic congestion. The city’s road network is extensive but historically strained. To address this, the municipal government has invested heavily in public transportation. One of the most notable developments is the Trans Jate (Jawa Timur Terpadu) Bus Rapid Transit (BRT) system, launched in 2014. Trans Jate offers an affordable, dedicated-lane bus service across the city’s core areas, providing a critical alternative to private car use. Additionally, the city utilizes the Djarum and KRL Commuter Line (Kawasan Industri Surabaya), which connects Surabaya with Sidoarjo and Mojokerto, facilitating daily commuting.

Air travel is facilitated by Juanda International Airport, located in the surrounding Sidoarjo regency but serving as the primary airport for Surabaya. Juanda is one of the busiest airports in Indonesia and a major hub for domestic and international flights, connecting Surabaya to key destinations in Asia, the Middle East, and Europe. The airport underwent a major expansion in the 2010s to handle increased passenger volumes and improve cargo facilities, reflecting the city’s growing importance as a regional logistics hub.

Rail transport is another critical component of Surabaya’s infrastructure. Surabaya Gubeng and Surabaya Pasarturi are the two main railway stations, serving as major nodes in the national railway network. These stations connect Surabaya to other major Javanese cities, including Jakarta, Semarang, and Banyuwangi, as well as to Bali via intercity trains like the Argo Bromo and Brawijaya. The railway system remains a popular choice for both passenger and freight transport. In addition to these modes, Surabaya has explored the implementation of a Light Rail Transit (LRT) system, although progress has been gradual due to funding and planning complexities. The city also maintains a network of rivers and canals, some of which have been rehabilitated for drainage and recreational purposes, such as the revitalization of the Surabaya River (Sungai Surabaya), which now serves as a linear park and cultural corridor.

Demographics and Social Dynamics

Surabaya’s population is a testament to the island of Java’s historical role as a crossroads of trade and migration. According to recent census data, the city’s population exceeds 3 million, with the Greater Surabaya metropolitan area housing over 10 million residents. The demographic composition is a mosaic of Javanese, Chinese-Indonesian, Madurese, Arab-Indonesian, and other ethnic groups. The Javanese community forms the largest ethnic group, followed by the Madurese, many of whom are involved in the fishing and maritime industries given their proximity to the Madura Strait.

The Chinese-Indonesian community has a deep historical presence in Surabaya, playing a crucial role in the city’s commercial development from the colonial era to the present day. Their contributions are evident in the city’s culinary landscape, business practices, and cultural institutions. The Arab-Indonesian community, primarily descended from traders from Hadramaut in Yemen, has also significantly influenced Surabaya’s culture, particularly in religious and commercial spheres. The Kampung Arab (Arab Quarter) near the Tunjungan Square is a historic testament to this presence.

Religiously, Surabaya is predominantly Muslim, with Islam being the dominant faith. However, the city is also home to significant communities of Christians (Protestant and Catholic), Hindus, and Buddhists, reflecting the pluralistic nature of Indonesian society. This religious diversity is generally managed through inter-faith dialogue and community organizations, although it has occasionally been a source of tension during periods of national political unrest. The social fabric of Surabaya is characterized by a strong sense of community and mutual aid, often organized through rural (village) and urban neighborhood associations. The local culture is known for its straightforwardness and resilience, traits that are celebrated in local folklore, literature, and the popular music genre campursari, which blends traditional Javanese, Chinese, and Western musical elements.
