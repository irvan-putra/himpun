Splinterwake, Chapter 3

The Loop

The air in the Spire of Ombra’s neutral guest quarters did not smell of the recycled ozone that permeated the Cartographer sectors, nor of the sterile lavender used in the Weaver’s clinics. It smelled of dust and old paper, a scent that Tamsin’s nose recognized as the smell of time settling. She stood by the window, watching the skyline of Ombra shift. A moment ago, the spire had been a brutalist concrete monolith; now, it was a gothic cathedral of black stone, the architecture bleeding and reforming as the three Earths it occupied argued for dominance.

She turned back to the room. Abbess Penultima sat in a high-backed chair that seemed too large for her frail frame, her hands resting on the armrests. The hands were terrifying. The skin was translucent, and beneath the surface, Tamsin could see the veins turning brown, crumbling like dry leaves before they even touched the fabric. This was Rust, the cost of the Chronoclade’s trade.

"You are vibrating, child," Penultima said. Her voice was not loud, but it resonated in Tamsin’s chest, a low hum that felt like a tuning fork struck against the bone. "Your guilt is a heavy thing to carry. It makes you unstable. Unanchored."

Tamsin kept her hand on the Anchor-Crystal in her pocket. It hummed against her thigh, a cold pulse that countered the heat of her own racing heart. "I didn't defect for comfort, Abbess. I defected because your predecessors killed three thousand people in Sector Four. I watched the sky turn to glass."

"The Stable Atlas requires sacrifice," Penultima replied, her eyes milky and ancient. "Order requires pruning. You were a variable that exceeded its tolerance. But variables can be corrected."

Kellan, who had been leaning against the far wall with his arms crossed, straightened up. His posture was relaxed, but Tamsin knew better; he was coiled, his eyes scanning the room’s geometry. He was the Unmoored, the man who didn't fully exist in any one timeline. To him, the room wasn't just a room; it was a grid of possibilities.

"The deal is simple, Tamsin," Kellan said, his voice low. "She wants the Crystal to anchor the Golden Moment. A single, perfect second where the Sundering never happened. If she pulls it off, the bleeding stops. The pain stops. You, me, everyone—we get our original lives back. Or so the theory goes."

"Or so the theory goes," Penultima agreed, smiling. A flake of skin detached from her cheek and drifted to the floor. She didn't flinch. "We have resources you cannot imagine, Tamsin. The Chronoclade does not bleed. We hold. We freeze. We preserve. Give us the Crystal, and we will weave you into the timeline as a hero, not a defector. Your name will be restored."

Tamsin looked at the window. The gothic spire was now dissolving into a shimmering data-stream, the architecture of a digital Earth overtaking the stone. It was beautiful and horrifying. She thought of the thousands she had left behind. If the Golden Moment worked, they would be alive. Would they be happy? Or would they be puppets in a world that refused to change?

"And if I say no?" Tamsin asked.

"Then you are a threat to continuity," Penultima said softly. "And the Chronoclade does not tolerate errors."

Kellan pushed off the wall. "She’s lying. Or at least, she’s holding back. Look at the light."

Tamsin looked up. The overhead lamp was flickering, but not with electricity. It was stuttering. A second ago, the light had been on. Then off. Then on. The pattern was wrong. It wasn't a frequency; it was a repetition.

Click. Click. Click.

The sound came from the floor. The marble tiles were shifting, but not sliding. They were resetting.

"Kellan," Tamsin whispered.

"I see it," Kellan said, his face pale. "A loop. It’s local. The room is trapped."

"How long?" Tamsin asked, pulling the Crystal out. The light from the stone flared, casting long, distorted shadows.

"Short," Kellan said, his voice tight. "We’re at the ten-second mark. We just reset."

Tamsin’s stomach dropped. "Reset? What do you mean, reset?"

"Look at your hand."

Tamsin looked down. The Anchor-Crystal was in her right hand. But she remembered putting it in her pocket. She reached into her pocket and found it empty. The memory of putting it in her pocket was gone, replaced by the memory of holding it. But the physical reality was still catching up.

"Time is repeating every ten seconds," Kellan said, moving toward the door. He reached for the handle. "But only for us. Or maybe for the room. We’re stuck in a pocket."

He turned the handle. The door was locked. He turned it again. Locked.

"Break it," Tamsin said.

"I can’t," Kellan said. "In a loop, cause and effect are suspended. The door is locked in the previous ten seconds. It will be locked again in the next ten. Unless we change the variable."

"How?"

"We have to do something that doesn't happen in the loop," Kellan said, turning to her. "We have to break the pattern. Penultima is setting the loop. She’s using the Crystal’s potential energy, even without it, to anchor the moment. She wants us to panic. She wants us to break. If we break, we’re easy to splice. Or to kill."

Penultima sat motionless, watching them with those milky eyes. "You are fighting the tide, Kellan. The tide does not care if you swim."

Tamsin’s mind raced. She was a Cartographer. She dealt in space, in folding and anchoring. This was time. But she knew that space and time were linked. If the time was looping, the space had to be stable. The room was a fixed point.

"The door," Tamsin said. "It’s locked because in the loop, you’re always trying to open it. You’re never actually getting through because the reset happens before you do."

"So?" Kellan asked.

"So we don't try to open it. We have to change the room before the reset."

Tamsin looked at the Crystal. It was hot in her hand. The air in the room was growing thick, the smell of dust intensifying. The clock on the mantel—no, there was no clock. There was only the rhythm of the light.

Click. Click. Click.

She had seconds. She had to do something spatial. Something that would disrupt the temporal anchor.

"Kellan, the window," Tamsin said.

"The window?"

"The view outside is shifting between three Earths. That means the spatial coordinates are unstable. If I fold the space inside this room, I can create a tear. A fracture line. A loop needs a closed circle. I’ll make the circle open."

"That requires an Anchor-Crystal," Kellan said. "And a massive amount of energy. If you do that, you could rip the room apart."

"I don't care," Tamsin said.

She slammed the Crystal against the wall.

Not to break it. To pin it.

She visualized the room, the air, the dust. She focused on the feeling of displacement sickness that had haunted her since the Sundering, the nausea of being in two places at once. She pushed that feeling into the Crystal, letting it bleed into the wall. The marble turned translucent, then transparent. The air shimmered.

Penultima stood up. For the first time, she looked afraid. "No. You are not a Weaver. You do not have the precision."

"I don't need precision," Tamsin gasped, her skin beginning to turn translucent at the edges, the cost of the spatial fold taking hold. "I need chaos."

Kellan moved with her. "I can't help you hold it, but I can hold the door. If the loop resets, I need to be in a state that doesn't exist in the previous cycle."

"Run," Kellan said.

"Where?"

"To the hallway. If the room loops, the hallway doesn't. The loop is local. We just need to be outside the boundary."

Tamsin gripped the Crystal, tearing it from the wall. The air in the room began to shriek. The furniture—the chair, the table, the window—began to blur. They were not moving; they were being unmade.

"Go!" Tamsin screamed.

Kellan didn't hesitate. He kicked the door. It didn't open; it dissolved. He grabbed Tamsin’s arm, pulling her. The floor beneath them became like water. They stumbled into the hallway, the air behind them freezing into a block of crystallized time.

They tumbled down the corridor, gasping, their feet slipping on the smooth floor. Behind them, the door to the guest quarters was gone. In its place was a jagged scar in the air, a swirling vortex of gray mist and shattered marble.

Tamsin collapsed against the wall, her arm shaking. Her fingers were fading, the skin becoming clear enough to see the bone beneath. The spatial fold had taken a toll.

Kellan checked his watch. A standard digital watch, useless in the void, but the numbers were ticking.

Ten. Nine. Eight.

"It worked," he panted. "The loop is broken. Or... moved."

Tamsin looked at the scar in the wall. Through the mist, she could see Penultima. The Abbess was standing in the doorway of the vanished room, her hands outstretched. Her body was crumbling, the Rust accelerating. She was smiling, but it was a look of defeat.

"You broke the moment," Penultima’s voice echoed, distorted by the spatial tear. "Now there is no Golden Moment. Only the bleed."

Tamsin looked at Kellan. "Did we do it? Did we break the plan?"

Kellan looked at the scar, then at Tamsin’s fading hand. "We bought time, Tamsin. But the Chronoclade doesn't forgive. And the Weavers are still in the Spire. We’re in the middle of a three-way war, and we just handed them a target."

Tamsin pulled her arm away from the fading light. She needed to move. She needed to get out of the Spire before the Cartographers noticed the disruption, before the Weavers tried to splice her again, and before the Chronoclade sent more Pruners.

"Where do we go?" Kellan asked.

Tamsin looked at the map of the Spire that was projected on the wall. The Spire was neutral ground, but it was also a prison. To get out, they had to go through the Splinterwake.

"The Glass Desert," Tamsin said.

Kellan stared at her. "That’s a graveyard. That’s where the failed experiments go. That’s where time is broken."

"Then it’s the only place they won't look," Tamsin said. "Because it’s dead."

Kellan looked at her, then at the scar in the wall. He nodded slowly. "The desert is to the south. But the path is guarded. By who, I don't know. But we have to move. Now."

They began to walk, deeper into the Spire, away from the crumbling ghost of the Abbess and the broken loop. The air still tasted of dust, but now, it tasted of possibility. And danger.
