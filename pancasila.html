Pancasila: The Philosophical Foundation of the Indonesian State

Pancasila is the foundational philosophical theory and state ideology of the Republic of Indonesia, serving as the guiding framework for the nation’s political, social, and legal structures. The term is derived from two Sanskrit words: panca, meaning "five," and silā, meaning "stone," "principle," or "base." Literally translating to "five stones" or "five pillars," the term implies a structure where each stone supports the others; if one is removed, the structure collapses. These five principles constitute the core ideological identity of the Indonesian state, designed to accommodate the country's vast diversity in ethnicity, religion, culture, and geography.

Unlike the constitutions of many other nations that rely primarily on strict legal positivism or a specific religious doctrine, Pancasila functions as sumber dari segala sumber hukum negara (the source of all sources of state law). It aims to unify the archipelagic nation under a common identity while acknowledging and respecting differences. The ideology is not merely a political slogan but is deeply integrated into the education system, public administration, and national rituals, acting as a moral compass for citizens and a check on state power. Its formulation was a complex historical process involving debates among nationalists, Islamic scholars, and Christian leaders in the early 1940s, resulting in a compromise that sought to balance secular governance with spiritual values, ensuring stability in a post-colonial state facing the threat of fragmentation.

Historical Formulation and the BPUPKI

The creation of Pancasila was a critical component of the preparation for Indonesian independence, taking place during the final years of the Japanese occupation of the Dutch East Indies. In 1943, the Japanese military administration established the Badan Penyelidik Usaha-Usaha Persiapan Kemerdekaan Indonesia (BPUPKI), or the Investigating Committee for Preparatory Work for Independence, to study the necessary conditions for independence. Although the Japanese initially intended to keep Indonesia as a puppet state within the "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere," the committee’s mandate to draft a constitution inadvertently provided the intellectual space for Indonesian leaders to define the nature of the future state.

The principal architect of Pancasila was Sukarno, who would later become the first President of Indonesia. On June 1, 1945, Sukarno delivered a seminal speech at the first BPUPKI meeting outlining his vision for the state’s foundation. He proposed five concepts: Nasionalisme Indonesia (Indonesian nationalism), Internasionalisme atau Perikemanusiaan (Internationalism or humanitarianism), Demokrasi (Democracy, which he referred to as mufakat or consensus), Kesejahteraan Rakyat (Welfare of the people), and Ketuhanan (Divinity). He named this collection of ideas "Pancasila," arguing that these five pillars were interconnected and formed a unified whole. However, Sukarno’s proposal did not immediately achieve consensus, particularly regarding the precise role of religion in the state.

The debate shifted on June 22, 1945, when Muhammad Yamin proposed a different set of five principles, which included Perikemanusiaan (Humanity), Persatuan (Unity), Rakyat Kuasa (The Sovereignty of the People), Kerakyatan (Democracy), and Keadilan Rakyat (Social Justice). While the wording differed, the themes were similar. The most contentious issue remained the first principle: whether the state should be explicitly Islamic or based on a broader monotheistic concept.

On June 22, 1945, a subcommittee of nine members, known as the Panitia Sembilan (Committee of Nine), was formed to mediate the dispute. The committee included representatives from the Islamic faction and the nationalist faction. After days of intense deliberation, they reached a compromise on June 22, 1945, later formalized in the draft preamble on June 22 and adopted by the full committee on June 22, 1945, though often historically dated to the session where the text was finalized for the full assembly. This document became known as the Jakarta Charter (Piagam Jakarta). The first principle was amended from the vague "Ketuhanan" to "Ketuhanan dengan kewajiban menjalankan syariat Islam bagi pemeluk-pemeluknya" (Belief in the One God with the obligation for the followers of Islam to carry out the Islamic Shari’ah). This formulation satisfied the Islamic nationalist faction, represented by figures such as Ki Bagus Hadikusumo and Wahid Hasyim, who argued that Islam should be the basis of the state, while the nationalist faction agreed to this specific clause to secure unity.

The Jakarta Charter Compromise and Final Adoption

The Jakarta Charter marked a pivotal moment in Indonesian history, representing a fragile consensus between secular nationalists and religious groups. However, as independence neared, representatives from the eastern islands of Indonesia, predominantly Christian and Hindu, raised serious concerns. They argued that the specific reference to Islamic Shari’ah would marginalize non-Muslim citizens and potentially lead to communal conflict, undermining the goal of national unity. This diplomatic pressure was applied to the new committee, the Panitia Persiapan Kemerdekaan Indonesia (PPKI) or Preparatory Committee for Indonesian Independence, tasked with refining the draft preamble to the constitution just before the proclamation.

On June 18, 1945, the Committee of Nine, led by Sukarno and including representatives from all major political and religious factions, had initially met to resolve the dispute. A key figure in this resolution was Soepomo, a Javanese lawyer and politician, who proposed a compromise that satisfied both sides without explicitly mentioning any single religion. He suggested the wording "Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa" (Belief in the One and Only God). This phrase was seen as theologically neutral enough to allow Muslims, Christians, Hindus, and Buddhists to interpret "God" according to their own faiths, while still affirming a monotheistic foundation for the state. The removal of the phrase "obligation to practice Islamic law" was the critical concession.

On August 18, 1945, one day before the proclamation of independence on August 17, the PPKI officially adopted the final version of the Preamble (Pembukaan) to the 1945 Constitution. The Preamble contains the five principles of Pancasila, which read as follows:

1. Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa (Belief in the One and Only God)

2. Kemanusiaan yang Adil dan Beradab (Just and Civilized Humanity)

3. Persatuan Indonesia (Unity of Indonesia)

4. Kerakyatan yang Dipimpin oleh Hikmat Kebijaksanaan dalam Permusyawaratan/Perwakilan (Democracy Guided by the Inner Wisdom in the Unanimity Arising Out of Deliberations Amongst Representatives)

5. Keadilan Sosial bagi Seluruh Rakyat Indonesia (Social Justice for All the People of Indonesia)

This final formulation removed the specific reference to Islamic Shari’ah found in the Jakarta Charter, ensuring that the state remained officially non-religious (though not secular in the Western sense of strict separation) and inclusive of all religious groups. This compromise is often cited as the cornerstone of Indonesian nation-building, allowing for a pluralistic state identity that could accommodate the majority Muslim population alongside significant minorities.

Legal Status and Constitutional Mechanisms

Pancasila is enshrined in the Preamble of the 1945 Constitution (UUD 1945), which holds the highest legal authority in Indonesia. The Preamble states that Pancasila is the source of all sources of law (sumber dari segala sumber hukum negara), meaning that no law, regulation, or state action can contradict its principles. This status grants Pancasila a quasi-constitutional supremacy that transcends specific statutory laws.

In the early years of the Republic, particularly during the Old Order era under Sukarno (1945–1966), the application of Pancasila was often interpreted through the lens of Guided Democracy. The state emphasized the role of the presidency in mediating between society and the market, often prioritizing the third principle (Unity) over the fourth (Democracy), leading to the suppression of parliamentary opposition.

During the New Order era under President Suharto (1966–1998), Pancasila was institutionalized through the P4 (Pedoman Penghayatan dan Pengamalan Pancasila) program. This was a mandatory training regime for civil servants, military personnel, and students to ensure ideological conformity. While criticized by human rights observers for being a tool of political control and suppressing dissent, this period also saw the deep integration of Pancasila into the national education curriculum, making it a core subject for all students from primary to university levels. The state promoted the concept of Pancasila Democracy, arguing that consensus-based governance was superior to Western liberal democracy.

Following the Reformasi (Reform) era, which began in 1998 after Suharto’s resignation, the legal status of Pancasila has undergone reinterpretation. The Constitutional Court (Mahkamah Konstitusi), established in 2003, plays a crucial role in adjudicating whether laws violate the spirit of Pancasila. The Court has ruled on issues ranging from the rights of non-Muslims to access religious symbols to the limits of state regulation on religious practice, always citing the Preamble as the ultimate standard. The constitutional amendments of 1999–2002 strengthened civil liberties, reflecting a shift from a state-centric interpretation of Pancasila to a more rights-based approach. This reinterpretation aligns the "Just and Civilized Humanity" and "Social Justice" pillars with international human rights standards, placing greater emphasis on individual rights while maintaining the collective moral framework.

State Religion and the Six Official Faiths

A unique feature of Pancasila in practice is the state’s role in regulating religion, derived from the first principle, Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa. While Indonesia has no official state religion, the state recognizes and regulates religious affairs to maintain social harmony. This creates a paradox: the state is not Islamic, yet it manages religious identity in a way that influences citizenship and public life.

All citizens are required to identify with one of six officially recognized religions in their civil identity cards (KTP): Islam, Protestantism, Catholicism, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Confucianism. This regulatory framework is a distinctive feature of Indonesian statecraft, balancing the need for social cohesion with religious freedom. The inclusion of Confucianism in 2000, after decades of struggle by the Confucian community during the anti-Communist purges of 1965, illustrates the dynamic nature of this system. The state views these religions as providers of moral and ethical guidance necessary for a stable society.

This regulatory approach has led to significant legal and social debates, particularly regarding the "Free Exercise of Religion" (KEB, Kebebasan Beragama). Critics argue that the state’s involvement in defining orthodoxy, such as through the Fatwa Councils or the Department of Religious Affairs, can marginalize syncretic or non-mainstream groups. For instance, the Ahmadiyya Muslim community and the Al-Ikhwan Islamic group have faced legal restrictions and social persecution, despite Pancasila’s guarantee of freedom of belief. The state often invokes the second and third principles (Humanity and Unity) to justify restrictions on religious expressions deemed to incite communal tension, highlighting the tension between Pancasila’s promise of liberty and its mandate for order.

Contemporary Challenges and Social Applications

In the 21st century, Pancasila faces challenges from resurgent political Islam, ethnic conflicts, and economic inequality. The ideology serves as the primary framework for addressing these issues. For example, during the inter-ethnic conflicts in Ambon (1999–2004) and later in Poso, the government invoked the third principle (Persatuan Indonesia) to promote reconciliation and interfaith dialogue. The "Pancasila Village" (Desa Pancasila) initiative, launched in the 2010s, aims to foster communities where different religious groups live and worship together, explicitly using Pancasila as a tool for grassroots peacebuilding.

Economically, the fifth principle (Social Justice) is used to justify state intervention in the economy to protect the poor and regulate foreign capital. This is seen in policies such as the Sistem Ekonomi Pancasila, which advocates for a mixed economy where private enterprise is tempered by social responsibility and state oversight to prevent excessive wealth concentration. The government frequently uses this principle to defend policies on labor rights, land redistribution, and the protection of local businesses against multinational corporations.

However, the application of Pancasila is not without controversy. Political opponents often accuse the ruling party of using Pancasila as a rhetorical shield to suppress criticism. For instance, accusations of "anti-Pancasila" have been leveled against various political groups, including Islamic activists and certain liberal intellectuals, suggesting that Pancasila has become a contested symbol rather than a unifying one. The challenge for the Indonesian state is to maintain Pancasila’s moral authority while adapting its implementation to a modern, pluralistic, and increasingly digital society.
