Pancasila: The Philosophical Foundation of the Indonesian State

Pancasila is the foundational philosophical theory of the Republic of Indonesia, serving as the guiding framework for the nation’s political, social, and legal structures. The term "Pancasila" is derived from two Sanskrit words: *panca*, meaning "five," and *silā*, meaning "principle" or "stone," literally translating to "five stones" or "five principles." These five principles constitute the core ideology of the Indonesian state, designed to accommodate the country's vast diversity in ethnicity, religion, culture, and geography. Unlike the constitutions of many other nations that rely primarily on legal positivism or a specific religious doctrine, Pancasila functions as a *sumber dari segala sumber hukum negara* (the source of all sources of state law). It aims to unify the archipelagic nation under a common identity while acknowledging and respecting differences. The ideology is not merely a political slogan but is deeply integrated into the education system, public administration, and national rituals, acting as a moral compass for citizens and a check on state power. Its formulation was a complex historical process involving debates among nationalists, Islamic scholars, and Christian leaders in the early 1940s, resulting in a compromise that sought to balance secular governance with spiritual values, ensuring stability in a post-colonial state facing the threat of fragmentation.

Historical Formulation and the BPUPKI

The creation of Pancasila was a critical component of the preparation for Indonesian independence, taking place during the final years of the Japanese occupation of the Dutch East Indies. In 1943, the Japanese military administration established the *Badan Penyelidik Usaha-Usaha Persiapan Kemerdekaan Indonesia* (BPUPKI), or the Investigating Committee for Preparatory Work for Independence, to study the necessary conditions for independence. Although the Japanese initially intended to keep Indonesia as a puppet state within the "Greater East Asia Co-Prosperity Sphere," the committee’s mandate to draft a constitution inadvertently provided the intellectual space for Indonesian leaders to define the nature of the future state.

The principal architect of Pancasila was Sukarno, who would later become the first President of Indonesia. On June 1, 1945, Sukarno delivered a speech at the first BPUPKI meeting outlining his vision for the state’s foundation. He proposed five concepts: *Nasionalisme Indonesia* (Indonesian nationalism), *Internasionalisme atau Perikemanusiaan* (Internationalism or humanitarianism), *Demokrasi* (Democracy, which he referred to as *mufakat*), *Kesejahteraan Rakyat* (Welfare of the people), and *Ketuhanan* (Divinity). He named this collection of ideas "Pancasila," arguing that these five pillars were interconnected and formed a unified whole. However, Sukarno’s proposal did not immediately achieve consensus, particularly regarding the role of religion.

The debate shifted on June 22, 1945, when Muhammad Yamin proposed a different set of five principles, which included *Perikemanusiaan*, *Persatuan*, *Rakyat Kuasa*, *Kerakyatan*, and *Keadilan Rakyat*. While the wording differed, the themes were similar. The most contentious issue remained the first principle. On July 2, 1945, the committee reached a compromise, known as the *Jakarta Charter* (*Piagam Jakarta*). The first principle was amended from "Ketuhanan" to "Ketuhanan dengan kewajiban menjalankan syariat Islam bagi pemeluk-pemeluknya" (Belief in the One God with the obligation for the followers of Islam to carry out the Islamic Shari’ah). This formulation satisfied the Islamic nationalist faction, represented by figures such as Ki Bagus Hadikusumo and Wahid Hasyim, who argued that Islam should be the basis of the state.

The Jakarta Charter Compromise and Final Adoption

The *Jakarta Charter* marked a pivotal moment in Indonesian history, representing a fragile consensus between secular nationalists and religious groups. However, as independence neared, representatives from the eastern islands of Indonesia, predominantly Christian and Hindu, raised concerns that the specific reference to Islamic Shari’ah would marginalize non-Muslim citizens and potentially lead to communal conflict. This diplomatic pressure was applied to the new committee, the *Panitia Sembilan* (Committee of Nine), tasked with refining the draft preamble to the constitution.

On June 18, 1945, the Committee of Nine, led by Sukarno and including representatives from all major political and religious factions, met to resolve the dispute. A key figure in this resolution was Soepomo, a Javanese lawyer, who proposed a compromise that satisfied both sides without explicitly mentioning any single religion. He suggested the wording "Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa" (Belief in the One and Only God). This phrase was seen as theologically neutral enough to allow Muslims, Christians, Hindus, and Buddhists to interpret "God" according to their own faiths, while still affirming a monotheistic foundation for the state.

On August 18, 1945, one day before the proclamation of independence, the *Panitia Persiapan Kemerdekaan Indonesia* (PPKI) or Preparatory Committee for Indonesian Independence, officially adopted the final version of the Preamble (*Pembukaan*) to the 1945 Constitution. The Preamble contains the five principles of Pancasila, which read as follows:

1. *Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa* (Belief in the One and Only God)

2. *Kemanusiaan yang Adil dan Beradab* (Just and Civilized Humanity)

3. *Persatuan Indonesia* (Unity of Indonesia)

4. *Kerakyatan yang Dipimpin oleh Hikmat Kebijaksanaan dalam Permusyawaratan/Perwakilan* (Democracy Guided by the Inner Wisdom in the Unanimity Arising Out of Deliberations Amongst Representatives)

5. *Keadilan Sosial bagi Seluruh Rakyat Indonesia* (Social Justice for All the People of Indonesia)

This final formulation removed the specific reference to Islamic Shari’ah found in the *Jakarta Charter*, ensuring that the state remained officially non-religious (though not secular in the Western sense) and inclusive of all religious groups.

Legal Status and Constitutional Mechanism

Pancasila is enshrined in the Preamble of the 1945 Constitution (*UUD 1945*), which holds the highest legal authority in Indonesia. The Preamble states that Pancasila is the source of all sources of law (*sumber dari segala sumber hukum negara*), meaning that no law, regulation, or state action can contradict its principles. In the early years of the Republic, particularly during the Old Order era under Sukarno (1945–1966), the application of Pancasila was often interpreted through the lens of *Golkar* (Functionary Groups) politics, which emphasized the role of the state in mediating between society and the market.

During the New Order era under President Suharto (1966–1998), Pancasila was institutionalized through the *P4* (Pedoman Pelaksanaan Penghayatan dan Pengamalan Pancasila) program. This was a mandatory training regime for civil servants, military personnel, and students to ensure ideological conformity. While criticized for being a tool of political control, this period also saw the integration of Pancasila into the national education curriculum, making it a core subject for all students from primary to university levels.

Following the *Reformasi* (Reform) era, which began in 1998 after Suharto’s resignation, the legal status of Pancasila has undergone reinterpretation. The Constitutional Court (*Mahkamah Konstitusi*) established in 2003 plays a crucial role in adjudicating whether laws violate the spirit of Pancasila. For example, the Court has ruled on issues ranging from the rights of non-Muslims to access religious symbols to the limits of state regulation on religious practice, always citing the Preamble as the ultimate standard. The state remains a unitary republic, but the constitutional amendment in 1999–2002 strengthened civil liberties, reflecting a shift from a state-centric interpretation of Pancasila to a more rights-based approach that aligns the "Just and Civilized Humanity" and "Social Justice" pillars with international human rights standards.

The Role of the State and Governance

The implementation of Pancasila in governance is characterized by a "guided democracy" model, though the term has evolved over time. The first principle, *Ketuhanan Yang Maha Esa*, mandates that the state recognizes religion but does not endorse a state religion. Consequently, Indonesia has no official state religion, yet the state actively regulates religious affairs. All citizens are required to identify with one of the six officially recognized religions: Islam, Protestantism, Catholicism, Hinduism, Buddhism, and Confucianism (recognized in 2000 after decades of struggle by the Confucian community). This regulatory framework is a unique feature of Indonesian statecraft, balancing the need for social order with religious freedom.

The third principle, *Persatuan Indonesia* (Unity of Indonesia), is central to the management of a nation comprising over 1,300 ethnic groups and 700 languages. The state promotes the use of Bahasa Indonesia (Indonesian) as the national language to foster a common identity, distinct from the "Bahasa Nasional" concept which is separate from local languages. Policies on decentralization since the 1990s have allowed for greater local autonomy, but the state retains control over strategic resources and security, ensuring that regional identities do not fragment the national unity.

The fourth principle, *Kerakyatan* (Democracy), originally referred to as *musyawarah mufakat* (deliberation to reach consensus). In practice, modern Indonesian democracy is a representative system with regular general elections. However, the cultural norm of consensus-seeking still influences political behavior, often prioritizing stability and harmony over adversarial political conflict. The fifth principle, *Keadilan Sosial* (Social Justice), drives state policies on wealth distribution, poverty alleviation, and social welfare. Programs such as the *Kartu Indonesia Pintar* (Indonesian Smart Card) for education subsidies and the *Bansos* (social aid) cash transfers are direct operationalizations of this principle, aiming to ensure that the benefits of economic growth are shared across all strata of society.

Education and Socialization

Pancasila is deeply embedded in the Indonesian educational system, known as *Pendidikan Pancasila*. It is a compulsory subject in all public and private schools. The curriculum has evolved over the decades. During the New Order, the focus was on *P4*, which was a highly structured, indoctrination-style program requiring students to undergo training camps. After 1998, the curriculum was revised to be more critical and analytical, encouraging students to understand the historical context and contemporary application of the principles.

Today, *Pendidikan Pancasila* is part of the Civics Education (*Pendidikan Kewarganegaraan*) framework. Students are taught to analyze case studies of social conflicts, environmental issues, and political disputes through the lens of the five principles. The goal is to produce "citizens who are aware of their rights and duties" (*warga negara yang sadar hak dan kewajibannya*). Beyond the formal curriculum, Pancasila is socialized through national rituals, such as the annual Independence Day celebrations on August 17, where the reading of the Proclamation and the Preamble is a central act. These rituals reinforce the emotional and symbolic connection between the citizen and the state, using Pancasila as a shared language of national identity.

The educational impact is significant in a nation where diversity can lead to tension. By framing diversity as a strength under the umbrella of *Bhinneka Tunggal Ika* (Unity in Diversity), the education system aims to cultivate a sense of belonging that transcends ethnic and religious lines. However, critics argue that the educational approach can sometimes be superficial, treating Pancasila as a set of static slogans rather than a dynamic philosophical system capable of addressing modern challenges such as digital ethics, climate change, and globalization.

Challenges and Contemporary Interpretations

In the contemporary era, Pancasila faces several challenges that test its relevance and resilience. One of the most significant is the rise of political Islam. While the first principle allows for Islamic influence, the interpretation of "Belief in the One and Only God" is contested between secularists and religious conservatives. The latter often advocate for stricter adherence to Islamic law (*Sharia*) in various aspects of life, including public morality and personal status law, which can clash with the pluralistic spirit of *Persatuan Indonesia* and *Kemanusiaan*.

Economic inequality remains a challenge for the fifth principle, *Keadilan Sosial*. Despite rapid economic growth, Indonesia still faces significant disparities between urban and rural areas and between the eastern and western parts of the archipelago. Critics argue that the state has not fully realized the promise of social justice, with wealth concentration in certain groups leading to social unrest and a perception of elite capture. The state’s response includes increased spending on infrastructure and social safety nets, but the gap in income and opportunity persists.

Additionally, the digital age has introduced new dimensions to the interpretation of Pancasila. Issues of misinformation, hate speech, and cybercrime challenge the principles of *Kemanusiaan yang Adil dan Beradab* (Just and Civilized Humanity) and *Kerakyatan* (Democracy). The government has implemented the *ITE Law* (Electronic Information and Transactions Law) to regulate online behavior, but this has also raised concerns about freedom of speech. The tension between maintaining social harmony (as per Pancasila) and protecting civil liberties is an ongoing debate in Indonesian society.

Regional Implementation and Local Variations

While Pancasila is a national ideology, its implementation can vary significantly across Indonesia’s regions due to local cultures and customs (*adat*). In Java, the dominant ethnic group, the emphasis is often on *rukun* (harmony) and deference to authority, which aligns with the consensus-based interpretation of *Kerakyatan*. In contrast, in regions with strong indigenous traditions, such as Bali or Kalimantan, the interplay between local customs and state law can be more complex. For instance, in Bali, Hindu religious practices are deeply integrated into daily life and public administration, reflecting a localized expression of *Ketuhanan*.

Decentralization, which began in the late 1990s, has allowed local governments to incorporate local values into their governance, provided they do not violate the national constitution and Pancasila. Some provinces have issued local regulations (*Perda*) that reflect specific cultural or religious norms. However, these regulations are subject to judicial review if they are perceived to violate the principles of unity or human rights. This creates a dynamic where Pancasila serves as a flexible framework that can accommodate local variations without fragmenting the national identity.

Notable examples of this regional adaptation include the handling of religious festivals and public holidays. While the national calendar recognizes holidays for the six official religions, local communities often observe additional festivals that are not nationally recognized. The state generally accommodates these practices under the umbrella of *Ketuhanan*, acknowledging the spiritual diversity of the nation while maintaining a unified civic calendar. This flexibility is a key mechanism by which Pancasila maintains its legitimacy in a diverse society.
